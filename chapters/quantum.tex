\chapter{Quantum sets and quantum graphs}
\label{ch:quantum-sets-and-graphs}

In this chapter we introduce the notions of quantum sets and quantum
graphs. These were first considered independently by
\textcite{duan13}, in the context of \emph{quantum channels}, and by
\textcite{weaver2010}, in the context of \emph{quantum relations}.
\textcite{musto18} later developed a rigorous, categorical framework
for dealing with quantum sets.

Here we mostly follow the theory developed in \textcite{tobolski26}
and \textcite{gromada22}.

\section{Quantum sets}
\label{sec:quantum-sets}

Let \( B \) be a \( C^{*} \)-algebra equipped with a faithful positive
functional \( \psi \). By the Gelfand--Naimark  theorem
(\textcite{blackadar}, II.6.4.10), \( B \) can be
realized concretely as a closed \( * \)-subalgebra of the bounded linear
operators \( B(\mathcal{H}) \) on some Hilbert space \( \mathcal{H} \).
When \( B \) is finite-dimensional, this abstract picture simplifies
considerably: rather than passing to \( \mathcal{H} \), we can
identify \( B \) with its own Hilbert space completion \( L^2(B,\psi) \),
by equipping \( B \) itself with the inner product induced by \( \psi \),
\[
    \langle x, y \rangle = \psi(x^{*}y), \qquad x, y \in B.
\]
Since \( \psi \) is faithful, this form is positive definite, and since
\( B \) is already finite-dimensional, it is automatically complete;
hence \( L^2(B,\psi) \) may simply be taken to be \( B \) itself, viewed
as a Hilbert space via this inner product.

This Hilbert space structure is the key extra piece of data that allows
one to speak of adjoints of maps built from \( B \), and in particular of
the adjoint of multiplication. This leads to the central definition of
this thesis, taken directly from \textcite{tobolski26}.

\begin{definition}[Quantum set, \textcite{tobolski26}, Definition 2.1]
    \label{def:quantum-set}
    A \emph{quantum set} \( (B, \psi) \) is a pair consisting of a
    finite-dimensional \( C^{*} \)-algebra \( B \) and a faithful positive
    functional \( \psi \colon B \rightarrow \mathbb{C} \) such that
    \[
        m m^{*} = \operatorname{Id}_{B}
    \]
    holds, where \( m \colon B \otimes B \rightarrow B \) is the
    multiplication map and \( m^{*} \) is its adjoint with respect to the
    inner products induced by \( \psi \), i.e.\ the unique linear map
    determined by
    \[
        \left\langle m(a \otimes b), c \right\rangle
        = \left\langle a \otimes b, m^{*}(c) \right\rangle_{\psi \otimes \psi}
    \]
    for all \( a, b, c \in B \).
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:quantum-set-functional}
    We refer to any functional \( \psi \) satisfying the condition in the
    definition above as a \emph{quantum set functional} on \( B \). The
    condition \( mm^{*} = \operatorname{Id}_B \) should be thought of as a
    normalization condition on \( \psi \): not every faithful positive
    functional on a finite-dimensional \( C^{*} \)-algebra makes
    \( (B,\psi) \) into a quantum set, only those compatible with the
    multiplication in this precise sense.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:comultiplication-general}
    Recall that, more generally, a \emph{comultiplication} on a
    algebra \( A \) is a coassociative linear comultiplication
    \[
        \Delta \colon A \rightarrow A \otimes A
    \]
    making the following diagram commute (coassociativity):
    \[
        \begin{tikzcd}
            A \arrow[r,"\Delta"] \arrow[d,"\Delta"']
            & A\otimes A \arrow[d,"{\Delta\otimes\mathrm{Id}}"] \\
            A\otimes A \arrow[r,"{\mathrm{Id}\otimes\Delta}"']
            & A\otimes A\otimes A
        \end{tikzcd}
    \]
    In general such a \( \Delta \) is far from unique, and need not
    interact with any given inner product on \( A \) at all. In our
    setting, however, the situation is much more rigid: once
    \( (B,\psi) \) is a quantum set, the adjoint \( m^{*} \) of the
    multiplication map is automatically a comultiplication on \( B \),
    and it is the \emph{unique} one arising this way, since it is pinned
    down by \( m \) together with the Riesz representation theorem for
    the Hilbert space \( L^2(B,\psi) \).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:m-star-notation}
    Because in our setup the comultiplication is obtained from the
    ordinary multiplication \( m \) purely by adjunction with respect to
    \( \psi \), we will not introduce separate notation for it, and will
    simply write \( m^{*} \) throughout.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Classical finite sets as quantum sets]
    \label{ex:classical_sets}
    Every finite set \( V \) gives rise to a quantum set
    \( (\mathbb{C}^{V}, \psi_V) \), where \( \mathbb{C}^{V} \)
    denotes the \( C^{*} \)-algebra of
    complex-valued functions on \( V \) with pointwise multiplication and
    involution given by pointwise conjugation,
    \( f^*(v) = \overline{f(v)} \), and where
    \[
        \psi_V \colon \mathbb{C}^{V} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}, \qquad
        \psi_V(f) = \sum_{v \in V} f(v).
    \]

    To see that \( \psi_V \) is positive, note that for any
    \( f \in \mathbb{C}^{V} \) the product \( f^*f \) evaluates pointwise as
    \[
        (f^*f)(v) = \overline{f(v)}\,f(v) = |f(v)|^2,
    \]
    then
    \[
        \psi_V(f^*f) = \sum_{v \in V} |f(v)|^2 \geq 0.
    \]

    For faithfulness, suppose \( \psi_V(f^*f) = 0 \) for some
    \( f \in \mathbb{C}^{V} \), i.e.
    \[
        \sum_{v \in V} |f(v)|^2 = 0.
    \]
    Since each summand \( |f(v)|^2 \) is non-negative, the sum can vanish
    only if every term does, so \( f(v) = 0 \) for all \( v \in V \), and
    hence \( f = 0 \). Thus \( \psi_V \) is faithful.

    It remains to check the quantum set condition \( mm^* =
    \operatorname{Id} \).
    Let \( \{e_v\}_{v \in V} \) denote the orthonormal basis of \(
    \mathbb{C}^{V} \)
    given by the characteristic functions of points. Since
    \( e_v e_w = \delta_{vw} e_v \) (pointwise multiplication of
    indicator functions is nonzero only when \( v = w \)), the
    multiplication map satisfies \( m(e_v \otimes e_w) = \delta_{vw} e_v \);
    dualizing this relation with respect to the inner product induced
    by \( \psi_V \) shows that \( m^* \) must send each basis vector
    \( e_v \) to the unique diagonal term \( e_v \otimes e_v \), i.e.
    \[
        m(f \otimes g) = fg, \qquad m^*(e_v) = e_v \otimes e_v.
    \]
    Evaluating the composite \( mm^* \) on a basis element gives
    \[
        (mm^*)(e_v) = m\big(m^*(e_v)\big) = m(e_v \otimes e_v) = e_v e_v = e_v,
    \]
    since \( e_v \) is idempotent. As \( mm^* \) and \( \operatorname{Id} \)
    agree on the basis \( \{e_v\}_{v \in V} \), they agree on all of
    \( \mathbb{C}^{V} \) by linearity, so \( mm^* = \operatorname{Id} \), and
    \( (\mathbb{C}^{V}, \psi_V) \) is indeed a quantum set.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:gelfand-duality}
    The identification of finite sets and commutative \(C^{*}\)-algebras is
    inspired by Gelfand's duality theorem~(\textcite{negrepontis71}),
    which establishes that
    every commutative \(C^{*}\)-algebra is isomorphic to the algebra of
    continuous functions on a compact Hausdorff space. In the
    finite-dimensional case, this space reduces to a discrete finite
    set, and the algebra's minimal projections correspond directly to
    the individual elements of the set. Furthermore, the functional \( \psi_X \)
    serves as a quantum analogue of the counting measure (summing
    over all elements), while the condition \( mm^{*} =
    \operatorname{Id} \) ensures the correct normalization and
    structural compatibility of the multiplication map with this
    measure, generalizing the discrete framework of
    classical sets to the quantum setting.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[The special case \( \mathbb{C}^n \)]
    \label{example:cn}
    Taking \( V = \{1, \dots, n\} \) in the previous example recovers the
    quantum set \( (\mathbb{C}^n, \psi_n) \), where \( \psi_n \) is
    determined on the standard basis by \( \psi_n(e_i) = 1 \) and extends
    linearly to
    \[
        \psi_n(v) = \sum_{i=1}^n v_i, \qquad v \in \mathbb{C}^n.
    \]
    Positivity and faithfulness follow exactly as above: writing
    \( v = \sum_i v_i e_i \) and using orthogonality of the basis,
    \[
        v^*v = \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n \overline{v_i}\,
        e_i\Big)\Big(\sum_{j=1}^n v_j e_j\Big)
        = \sum_{i=1}^n \overline{v_i}v_i\, e_i = \sum_{i=1}^n |v_i|^2 e_i,
    \]
    so that
    \[
        \psi_n(v^*v) = \sum_{i=1}^n |v_i|^2 \psi_n(e_i) =
        \sum_{i=1}^n |v_i|^2 \geq 0,
    \]
    which vanishes precisely when every \( v_i = 0 \), i.e.\ when
    \( v = 0 \). The quantum set condition again reduces to the
    computation
    \[
        (mm^*)(e_i) = m(e_i \otimes e_i) = e_i, \qquad i = 1,\dots,n,
    \]
    which forces \( mm^* = \operatorname{Id} \) by linearity.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Matrix algebras]
    \label{example:matrix-algebras-quantum-set}
    For any \( n \in \mathbb{N}_+ \), the pair
    \( (M_n (\mathbb{C}), n\operatorname{Tr}) \) is a quantum set, where
    \( \operatorname{Tr} \) denotes the usual trace on \( M_n
    (\mathbb{C}) \). We will
    later see that this quantum set arises naturally as a crossed
    product: writing \( \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \) for the dual of
    \( \mathbb{Z}_n \) acting on \( (\mathbb{C}^n, \psi_n) \) via the
    standard action, one has
    \[
        (M_n (\mathbb{C}), n\operatorname{Tr}) \;\cong\;
        (\mathbb{C}^n \rtimes \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n,\ \psi_\rtimes),
    \]
    a fact we prove in \ref{lem:iso}.
    \( n \operatorname{Tr} \) is chosen instead of simply \(
    \operatorname{Tr} \) so that the condition \( mm^{*} =
    \operatorname{Id} \) is fulfilled.
\end{example}

\begin{lemma}[Comultiplication for matrix algebras]
    \label{lem:comultiplication-matrix-algebras}
    In the quantum set \( (M_n (\mathbb{C}), n\operatorname{Tr}) \), the
    comultiplication \( m^* \) is given on matrix units by
    \[
        m^*(e_{ij}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n e_{ik} \otimes e_{kj},
    \]
    where \( e_{ij} \) denotes the matrix with a single \( 1 \) in the
    \( i \)-th row and \( j \)-th column, extended linearly to all of
    \( M_n (\mathbb{C}) \).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    The proof is a direct computation using two standard facts about
    matrix units:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \( e_{ab}e_{cd} = \delta_{bc}\, e_{ad} \);
        \item \( \langle e_{ij}, e_{kl} \rangle =
            n\operatorname{Tr}(e_{ji}e_{kl}) = n\,\delta_{ik}\,\delta_{jl} \),
    \end{enumerate}
    where \( \delta_{ij} \) is the Kronecker delta. In particular, (2)
    shows that \( \{e_{ij}\}_{i,j=1,\dots,n} \) forms an orthogonal
    (though not orthonormal, since \( \langle e_{ij},e_{ij}\rangle = n \))
    basis of \( M_n (\mathbb{C}) \) with respect to the inner product induced by
    \( n\operatorname{Tr} \).

    Fix arbitrary basis vectors \( e_{ab}, e_{cd} \). By (1),
    \[
        m(e_{ab} \otimes e_{cd}) = e_{ab}e_{cd} = \delta_{bc}\, e_{ad},
    \]
    and hence, for any basis vector \( e_{ij} \), using (2),
    \[
        \big\langle m(e_{ab} \otimes e_{cd}),\, e_{ij} \big\rangle
        = \delta_{bc} \big\langle e_{ad}, e_{ij} \big\rangle
        = \delta_{bc}\, n\,\delta_{ai}\,\delta_{dj}.
    \]
    On the other hand, pairing \( e_{ab} \otimes e_{cd} \) against the
    proposed adjoint gives
    \[
        \Big\langle e_{ab} \otimes e_{cd},\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n e_{ik}
        \otimes e_{kj} \Big\rangle
        = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n \langle e_{ab}, e_{ik} \rangle \langle e_{cd},
        e_{kj} \rangle
        = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n
        \big(n\,\delta_{ai}\delta_{bk}\big)\big(n\,\delta_{ck}\delta_{dj}\big).
    \]
    The sum over \( k \) collapses to the single term \( k = b = c \),
    so this equals
    \[
        \frac{1}{n}\cdot n^2\, \delta_{ai}\,\delta_{dj}\,\delta_{bc}
        = n\,\delta_{ai}\,\delta_{bc}\,\delta_{dj}.
    \]
    The two expressions coincide, so
    \[
        \big\langle m(e_{ab}\otimes e_{cd}), e_{ij} \big\rangle
        = \big\langle e_{ab}\otimes e_{cd}, m^*(e_{ij}) \big\rangle
    \]
    for all basis vectors, and since \( \{e_{ij}\} \) spans \( M_n
    (\mathbb{C}) \),
    this identifies \( m^* \) as claimed on the whole of \( M_n (\mathbb{C}) \)
    by linearity.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Crossed product quantum sets}
\label{subsec:crossed-product-quantum-sets}

\textcite{tobolski26} introduced the notion of a \emph{crossed product
quantum set}. Suppose we are given a finite-dimensional
\( C^{*} \)-algebra \( \widetilde{B} \) together with a dual group action
\( \widehat\alpha \colon \widehat{G} \to
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{B}) \), where
\( G \) is a finite abelian group. By
\ref{prop:finite-abelian-self-dual}, \( G \cong \widehat{G} \). One may
then form the crossed
product
\[
    B = \widetilde{B} \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{G}.
\]
If \( \widetilde{B} \) is equipped with a faithful functional
\( \widetilde\psi \) making \( (\widetilde{B}, \widetilde\psi) \) a
quantum set, one
can construct a corresponding faithful functional on \( B \) making
\( B \) into a quantum set as well, as follows.

\begin{definition}[Crossed product quantum set, Definition 3.1]
    \label{def:crossed-product-quantum-set}
    Let \( (\widetilde{B}, \widetilde\psi) \) be a quantum set, and let
    \( \widehat\alpha \colon \widehat{G} \to \operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{B},
    \widetilde\psi) \)
    be an action of the dual of a finite abelian group \( G \). The
    \emph{crossed product quantum set} is defined as
    \[
        (\widetilde{B}, \widetilde\psi) \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{G}
        = \big( \widetilde{B} \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha}
        \widehat{G},\ \psi_\rtimes \big),
    \]
    where \( \widetilde{B} \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{G} \) is
    the crossed
    product \( C^{*} \)-algebra, and \( \psi_\rtimes \) is the
    functional given by
    \[
        \psi_\rtimes\Big( \sum_{\chi \in \widehat{G}} b_\chi u_\chi \Big)
        = n\, \widetilde\psi(b_1),
    \]
    with \( n = |\widehat{G}| \).
\end{definition}

The proof that this definition is well posed, i.e. that the resulting
object is indeed a quantum set, can be found in
\textcite[Proposition~3.4]{tobolski26}.

\begin{example}[Example 3.5]
    \label{example:crossed-product-trivial-action}
    If \( \widehat\alpha = \operatorname{triv} \) is the trivial action, then
    \[
        \widetilde{B} \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{G} \;\cong\;
        \widetilde{B} \otimes C(G),
    \]
    via the isomorphism \( b_\chi u_\chi \mapsto b_\chi \otimes
    \widetilde u_\chi \),
    where \( \widetilde u_\chi \in C(G) \) denotes the function
    \( g \mapsto \chi(g) \) on \( G \).
\end{example}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:iso}
    Let \( \widehat\alpha \colon \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \to
    \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^n) \)
    be the action given by cyclically shifting coordinates. Then
    \[
        (M_n (\mathbb{C}), n\operatorname{Tr}) \;\cong\; (\mathbb{C}^n
        \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n,\ \psi_\rtimes).
    \]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Identify \( \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \) with \( \mathbb{Z}_n =
    \{0,1,\dots,n-1\} \) (arithmetic mod \( n \)), and write
    \( \{\delta_i\}_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_n} \) for the standard basis of
    \( \mathbb{C}^n \), so that \( \widehat\alpha_k(\delta_i) = \delta_{i+k} \).
    Since \( \mathbb{Z}_n \) is finite, every element of
    \( \mathbb{C}^n \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \) is a
    finite sum \( \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_n} b_k u_k \) with \( b_k \in
    \mathbb{C}^n \);
    writing each \( b_k = \sum_i b_k(i)\,\delta_i \) in coordinates, this
    algebra is spanned, as a vector space, by the \( n^2 \) products
    \[
        \delta_i u_k, \qquad i,k \in \mathbb{Z}_n.
    \]
    It therefore suffices to define a \( * \)-isomorphism
    \( \pi \colon \mathbb{C}^n \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha}
    \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \to M_n (\mathbb{C}) (\mathbb{C}) \)
    on this spanning set, check it respects the algebra's product and
    involution formulas on \( \delta_i u_k \), and then extend linearly.

    \textbf{Definition of \( \pi \).} Let \( e_{ab} \in M_n (\mathbb{C})
    (\mathbb{C}) \) denote
    the matrix unit sending \( \delta_b \mapsto \delta_a \) and every
    other \( \delta_c \mapsto 0 \), and set
    \[
        \pi(\delta_i u_k) = e_{i,\,i-k}, \qquad i,k \in \mathbb{Z}_n,
    \]
    extended linearly to all of \( \mathbb{C}^n
    \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha}\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \).
    Equivalently, writing \( S \) for the cyclic shift matrix
    \( S\delta_i = \delta_{i+1} \) and \( M_{b_k} = \sum_i b_k(i)\,e_{ii} \)
    for the diagonal matrix with entries \( b_k \), one checks directly
    that \( \pi(\delta_i u_k) = M_{\delta_i}S^k \), so this agrees with
    the general formula \( \pi\big(\sum_k b_k u_k\big) = \sum_k M_{b_k}S^k \).

    \textbf{\( \pi \) is a \( * \)-homomorphism.} It suffices to check
    the product and involution formulas on the spanning elements
    \( \delta_i u_k \). For the product, recall that
    \( u_k \delta_i u_k^* = \widehat\alpha_k(\delta_i) = \delta_{i+k} \) in
    \( \mathbb{C}^n \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha}\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \), matching
    \( S^k M_{\delta_i} S^{-k} = M_{\delta_{i+k}} \) in \( M_n (\mathbb{C})
    (\mathbb{C}) \), so
    \[
        \pi(\delta_i u_k)\,\pi(\delta_j u_l)
        = e_{i,i-k}\, e_{j,j-l}
        = \delta_{i-k,\,j}\; e_{i,\,j-l},
    \]
    using \( e_{ab}e_{cd} = \delta_{bc}e_{ad} \); this is nonzero
    exactly when \( j = i-k \), in which case it equals
    \( e_{i,\,i-k-l} = \pi(\delta_i u_{k+l}) \). On the other side,
    \[
        (\delta_i u_k)(\delta_j u_l) = \delta_i\,
        \widehat\alpha_k(\delta_j)\, u_{k+l}
        = \delta_i\,\delta_{j+k}\,u_{k+l} = \delta_{i,\,j+k}\,\delta_i\,u_{k+l},
    \]
    which is likewise nonzero exactly when \( j = i-k \), and then
    equals \( \delta_i u_{k+l} \). Applying \( \pi \) recovers
    \( \pi(\delta_i u_{k+l}) = e_{i,i-k-l} \), matching the computation
    above. So \( \pi \) respects multiplication on the spanning set,
    hence on all of \(
    \mathbb{C}^n\rtimes_{\widehat\alpha}\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \)
    by bilinearity.

    For the involution, \( (\delta_i u_k)^* =
        \widehat\alpha_{-k}(\delta_i^*)u_{-k}
    = \delta_{i-k}\,u_{-k} \), so \( \pi\big((\delta_i u_k)^*\big) =
    \pi(\delta_{i-k}u_{-k}) = e_{i-k,\,i} \), while directly
    \( \pi(\delta_i u_k)^* = e_{i,i-k}^* = e_{i-k,i} \), matching.

    \textbf{\( \pi \) is bijective.} As \( i,k \) range over
    \( \mathbb{Z}_n \), the pair \( (i,i-k) \) ranges exactly once over
    all of \( \mathbb{Z}_n\times\mathbb{Z}_n \) (fix \( i \); varying
    \( k \) makes \( i-k \) range over all residues), so
    \( \{\pi(\delta_i u_k)\}_{i,k} = \{e_{ab}\}_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_n} \)
    is exactly the full set of matrix units, a basis of \( M_n
    (\mathbb{C}) (\mathbb{C}) \).
    Since \( \{\delta_i u_k\}_{i,k} \) is a basis of
    \( \mathbb{C}^n\rtimes_{\widehat\alpha}\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \) of the
    same dimension \( n^2 \), and \( \pi \) carries it bijectively to a
    basis of \( M_n (\mathbb{C}) (\mathbb{C}) \), the linear map \(
    \pi \) is a bijection.

    \textbf{\( \pi \) intertwines the functionals.} Since a matrix
    unit \( e_{ab} \) has trace \( \delta_{ab} \),
    \[
        \operatorname{Tr}\big(\pi(\delta_i u_k)\big) =
        \operatorname{Tr}(e_{i,i-k}) = \delta_{i,i-k} = \delta_{k,0}.
    \]
    Hence for a general element \( b=\sum_k b_k u_k =
    \sum_{k,i}b_k(i)\,\delta_i u_k \),
    \[
        \operatorname{Tr}(\pi(b)) = \sum_{k,i}b_k(i)\,\delta_{k,0}
        = \sum_i b_0(i) = \widetilde\psi(b_0),
    \]
    where \( \widetilde\psi(x) = \sum_i x_i \) is the quantum set functional
    on \( (\mathbb{C}^n,\widetilde\psi) \), and \( b_0 \) is exactly the
    coefficient at the trivial character appearing in the definition of
    \( \psi_\rtimes \). Multiplying by \( n = |\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n| \),
    \[
        n\operatorname{Tr}(\pi(b)) = n\,\widetilde\psi(b_0) = \psi_\rtimes(b).
    \]

    Thus \( \pi \) is a \( * \)-isomorphism
    \( \mathbb{C}^n\rtimes_{\widehat\alpha}\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \to
        M_n (\mathbb{C})
    (\mathbb{C}) \)
    satisfying \( n\operatorname{Tr}\circ\pi = \psi_\rtimes \), which is
    exactly an isomorphism of quantum sets
    \[
        (\mathbb{C}^n \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha}
        \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_n,\ \psi_\rtimes)
        \;\cong\; (M_n (\mathbb{C}) (\mathbb{C}),\ n\operatorname{Tr}). \qedhere
    \]
\end{proof}

\section{Quantum graphs}
\label{sec:quantum-graphs}

A graph is classically defined as a pair \( (V, E) \) with \( V \)
serving as the \emph{set of vertices} and \( E \subseteq V^{2} \)
serving as the \emph{set of edges}. Such a graph could be interpreted
as a matrix \( A = [a_{ij}] \in \{0, 1\}^{n \times n} \) with \(
(v_{i}, v_{j}) \in E \Leftrightarrow a_{ij} = 1 \); such a
property of such matrices is that they are invariant with respect to
representation is often called the \emph{adjacency matrix},
see~\ref{ex:adj-matrix}. The key entrywise multiplication. In turn,
we can use this property to generalise graphs on sets to graphs on quantum sets.

\begin{example}
    \label{ex:adj-matrix}
    Consider the following directed graph on three vertices,
    featuring a bidirectional connection and a self-loop:

    \input{graphs/example_adj_matrix}

    Ordering the basis by \( \{v_0, v_1, v_2\} \), the corresponding
    \( 3 \times 3 \) adjacency matrix \( A \) is:
    \[
        A =
        \begin{pmatrix}
            0 & 1 & 0 \\
            1 & 0 & 1 \\
            0 & 0 & 1
        \end{pmatrix}.
    \]

    This linear representation is foundational when generalizing to
    quantum graphs, where the adjacency matrix \( A \in
    M_n(\mathbb{C}) \) is replaced by a linear map between \(C^{*}\)-algebras.
\end{example}

The following definitions are due to \textcite{musto18}. We follow
the approach of \textcite{tobolski26}.

\begin{definition}[Quantum adjacency matrix, Definition 2.5]
    \label{def:quantum-adjacency-matrix}
    Let \( (B, \psi) \) be a quantum set. A \emph{quantum adjacency
    matrix} on this quantum set is a linear map \( A\colon B \rightarrow B
    \) which is Schur-idempotent and *-preserving, by which we mean
    that it satisfies
    \[
        m(A \otimes A)m^{*} = A,
        \qquad
        A(b^{*}) = A(b)^{*}
        \quad \text{for all } b \in B.
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Quantum graph, Definition 2.5]
    \label{def:quantum-graph}
    A \emph{quantum graph} on \( (B, \psi) \) is given by a quantum
    adjacency matrix on this quantum set.
\end{definition}

For all intents and purposes, we will identify both notions and simply
call an appropriate \( A\colon B \rightarrow B \) both a quantum
adjacency matrix and a quantum graph.

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:other-quantum-graph-definitions}
    There are other equivalent definitions of a quantum graph, namely those
    based on
    \begin{itemize}
        \item an \emph{edge projection}, i.e. a projection \(
            P \in B \otimes B^{\operatorname{op}} \), see
            \textcite{musto18},
        \item a \( B' \) -- \( B' \)-bimodule \( S \subseteq
            B(\mathcal{H}) \), see \textcite{weaver2010}.
            % \item linear subspaces \( V \subseteq M_{3}(\mathbb{C}) \), see
            %     \textcite{gromada22}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Definition~4.6]
    \label{def:quantum-loop-free}
    Let \( A \) be a quantum graph on a quantum set \( (B, \psi) \).
    We call the quantum graph
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{loop-free} if \( m(A \otimes
            \operatorname{Id})m^{*} = 0, \)
        \item \emph{undirected} if \( A = A^{*} \), where the adjoint
            is taken with respect to the inner product induced by
            \( \psi \).
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:loop-free-motivation}
    The motivation behind the first point in the definition above
    will become clear thanks to \ref{lem:correspondences}, which says
    that \( m(A \otimes B)m^* \) can be thought of as taking the
    intersection of two graphs edgewise. Subsequently \( m(A \otimes
    \operatorname{Id})m^* \) would keep only loops, i.e. edges going
    from a vertex to itself. The second point is easy to motivate in
    a similar manner: a graph is undirected if and only if its
    adjacency matrix is symmetric.
\end{remark}

\section{Voltage and Derived Quantum Graphs}
\label{sec:voltage-and-derived-quantum-graphs}

In this section, we introduce the notions of voltage and derived
quantum graphs. Voltage graphs and their derived graphs were first
introduced by \textcite{gross74}; these notions have since
been extended to the setting of quantum graphs by \textcite{tobolski26}.

\subsection{Classical voltage graphs}
\label{subsec:classical-voltage-graphs}

Before turning to the quantum setting, it is instructive to first
recall the classical construction. Informally, a voltage graph is
simply a graph whose edges have been labeled, or \emph{weighted}, by
elements of a finite group; the derived graph then unfolds this
labeled graph into a (typically much larger) covering graph, built by
tracking how these group elements accumulate as one walks along paths
in the original graph. This construction originates in topological
graph theory~\cite{gross87}, where it was used to build graph
embeddings on surfaces of higher genus, but it also turns out to
provide a rich and flexible source of examples of graphs with
interesting symmetry, which is the
perspective we adopt here.

\begin{definition}[Multigraph, \textcite{diestel25}, Section 1.10]
    \label{def:multigraph}
    A \emph{multigraph} \( \Gamma = (V,E) \) is a graph in which
    multiple distinct edges may join the same pair of vertices, i.e.\
    \( E \) is a multiset on \( V \times V \) rather than a subset.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Classical voltage graph, \textcite{gross87}, Section 2.1]
    \label{def:classical-voltage-graph}
    A \emph{voltage graph} is a pair \( (\Gamma, \lambda) \) consisting
    of a finite directed multigraph \( \Gamma \),
    with vertex set \( V \) and edge set \( E \), together with a
    labelling \( \lambda \colon E \to G \) of the edges of \( \Gamma \)
    by elements of a finite group \( G \).
\end{definition}

From every voltage graph one can construct a new graph, called its
\emph{derived graph}, as follows.

\begin{definition}[Classical derived graph, \textcite{gross87}, Section 2.1]
    \label{def:classical-derived-graph}
    The \emph{derived graph} \( \Gamma^\lambda \) of \( (\Gamma,\lambda) \)
    has vertex set \( V \times G \) and edge set \( E \times G \).
    Whenever \( e \in E \) is an edge from \( u \) to \( v \) in
    \( \Gamma \), and \( g \in G \) is a group element, the
    corresponding edge \( (e,g) \) in \( \Gamma^\lambda \) runs from
    \( (u,g) \) to \( (v,\, g\lambda(e)) \).
\end{definition}

Intuitively, the derived graph consists of \( |G| \) labeled copies
(\emph{fibers}) of the vertex set of \( \Gamma \), one for each group
element, and every edge \( e \) of \( \Gamma \) is lifted to \( |G| \)
edges in \( \Gamma^\lambda \), one leaving each fiber, whose endpoint
is determined by translating within the target fiber according to the
voltage \( \lambda(e) \). This is precisely the picture underlying the
examples given below.

The following is a major result in the theory of voltage and derived
graphs, showing that this construction is not merely a convenient way
of producing examples, but in fact accounts for \emph{every} graph
admitting a free action of a finite group.

\begin{definition}[Graph automorphism, \textcite{diestel25}, Section 1.1]
    \label{def:graph-automorphism}
    Let \( \Gamma = (V, E) \) be a graph. An \emph{automorphism} of
    \( \Gamma \) is a bijection \( f \colon V \to V \) such that
    \[
        (u,v) \in E \iff (f(u), f(v)) \in E
    \]
    for all \( u, v \in V \), i.e.\ a permutation of the vertex set
    that preserves adjacency. The set of all automorphisms of
    \( \Gamma \), equipped with composition, forms a group, denoted
    \( \operatorname{Aut}(\Gamma) \).
\end{definition}

% TODO: give a reference
\begin{definition}[Group action on a graph]
    \label{def:group-action-on-graph}
    Let \( \Gamma = (V,E) \) be a graph and let \( G \) be a group. A
    \emph{group action} of \( G \) on \( \Gamma \) is a group
    homomorphism
    \[
        \alpha \colon G \to \operatorname{Aut}(\Gamma).
    \]
    As with group actions on \( C^{*} \)-algebras, we write \( g \cdot v \)
    instead of \( \alpha(g)(v) \) when the action in question is clear.
    The action is called \emph{free} if \( g \cdot v = v \) for some
    \( v \in V \) implies \( g = e_G \).
\end{definition}

% TODO: give a reference
\begin{definition}[Quotient graph]
    \label{def:quotient-graph}
    Let \( \Gamma = (V,E) \) be a graph and let \( G \) act on
    \( \Gamma \). The \emph{quotient graph} \( \Gamma/G \) is the
    graph with vertex set \( V/G \), the set of orbits of \( G \) on
    \( V \), and with an edge from the orbit \( [u] \) to the orbit
    \( [v] \) whenever \( (u',v') \in E \) for some
    \( u' \in [u] \), \( v' \in [v] \).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Gross and Tucker theorem, \textcite{gross87}, Theorem 2.2.2]
    \label{thm:gross-and-tucker}
    Let \( \widetilde\Gamma \) be a graph and let \( G \) be a finite group
    acting freely on \( \widetilde\Gamma \) by automorphisms. Then there
    exists a voltage graph \( (\Gamma,\lambda) \), with edges labeled
    by elements of \( G \), such that \( \widetilde\Gamma \) is isomorphic
    to the derived graph of \( (\Gamma,\lambda) \). The graph
    \( \Gamma \) is the quotient graph \( \widetilde\Gamma / G \).
\end{theorem}

In other words, voltage graphs classify free group actions on graphs
up to isomorphism: recovering \( \Gamma \) from
\( \widetilde\Gamma \) amounts to nothing more than quotienting by the
group action. However, this is not enough to recover the voltage labelling
\( \lambda \), as different labellings might create isomorphic derived graphs.

\subsection{Generalisation to Voltage Quantum and Derived Quantum Graphs}
\label{subsec:quantum-voltage-and-derived-graphs}

We now turn to generalising the Gross--Tucker construction to the
setting of quantum graphs. This generalisation was first carried out
by \textcite{tobolski26}, building on the notion
of a crossed product quantum set introduced earlier in this chapter.
Just as a classical voltage graph is a labelling of the edges of a
graph by group elements, a voltage \emph{quantum} graph will be a
family of quantum adjacency matrices indexed by the group \( G \)
rather than a single labelling function; the derived quantum graph
then reassembles this family into a single quantum adjacency matrix on
the crossed product quantum set \( (B, \psi_\rtimes) \), playing the
role of the derived graph \( \Gamma^\lambda \) from the classical
picture. The following definition makes this precise.

\begin{definition}[\textcite{tobolski26}, Definition 4.1]
    \label{def:quantum-voltage-graph}
    Suppose \( (\widetilde{B}, \widetilde\psi) \), the action
    \( \widehat\alpha \colon \widehat{G} \to \operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{B},
    \widetilde\psi) \),
    the crossed product \( (B, \psi_\rtimes) \), and the elements
    \( (u_\chi)_{\chi \in \widehat{G}} \subseteq B \) are as introduced
    in \ref{subsec:crossed-product-cstar}.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item For crossed product quantum set \( \widetilde{B}
            \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{G} \)
            we define the linear map
            \[
                E_{\rtimes}: \widetilde{B} \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{G}
                \rightarrow \widetilde{B}, \qquad \sum_{\chi \in \widehat{G}}
                b_{\chi} u_{\chi} \mapsto n b_{1},
            \]
            and my \( E_{\rtimes}^{*} \) its adjoint with respect to
            the inner products on \( \widetilde{B} \) and \( \widetilde{B}
            \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{G} \).
        \item For each \( g \in G \), set
            \[
                X_g = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{\chi \in \widehat{G}}
                \overline{\chi(g)}\, u_\chi u_\chi^*,
            \]
            i.e.\ let \( X_g \) be the linear operator
            \[
                X_g \colon B \to B, \qquad
                b \mapsto \frac{1}{n} \sum_{\chi \in \widehat{G}}
                \overline{\chi(g)}\, \langle u_\chi, b
                \rangle_{\psi_\rtimes}\, u_\chi.
            \]
        \item Given the group action
            \( \widehat\alpha \colon \widehat{G} \to
            \operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{B}, \widetilde\psi) \),
            a \emph{voltage quantum graph} on \( (\widetilde{B},
            \widetilde\psi) \)
            is a \( G \)-indexed family
            \( \widetilde{A} = (\widetilde{A}_g)_{g \in G} \) of quantum
            adjacency matrices such that
            \[
                \widetilde{A}_g \widehat\alpha_\chi = \widehat\alpha_\chi
                \widetilde{A}_g
            \]
            holds for every \( g \in G \) and every \( \chi \in \widehat{G} \).
        \item Given such a family \( \widetilde{A} \), the associated
            \emph{derived quantum graph}
            \( \widetilde{A} \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{G}
            \colon B \to B \)
            on \( (B, \psi_\rtimes) \) is
            \[
                \widetilde{A} \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{G}
                = \sum_{g \in G} m\big( X_g \otimes E_\rtimes^*
                \widetilde{A}_g E_\rtimes \big)\, m^*,
            \]
            where \( m = m_\rtimes \) is the multiplication map on \( B \).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The role played by \( X_g \) here is directly analogous to the
indicator function of the fiber \( V \times \{g\} \) in the classical
derived graph: it isolates, within \( B \), the \emph{copy of \( g \)}
sitting inside the crossed product. The fact that \( X_g \) is itself
an adjacency matrix on the quantum set \( (B,\psi_\rtimes) \) is
checked in \textcite[Proposition~4.5]{tobolski26}, and the derived
quantum graph is then built by combining these fiber projections with
the individual voltage components \( \widetilde{A}_g \), summed over all
\( g \in G \) -- mirroring how, in the classical construction, each
edge of \( \Gamma^\lambda \) is assigned both to a specific fiber and
to a specific lift of an edge of \( \Gamma \).

As in the classical case, it will be useful to know when a voltage
quantum graph gives rise to a derived quantum graph with no loops, or
one that is undirected.

\begin{definition}
    \label{def:voltage-quantum-graph-loop-free}
    A voltage quantum graph \( \widetilde{A} = \{\widetilde{A}_g\}_{g \in G} \)
    is called \emph{loop-free} if \( \widetilde{A}_{1} \) is loopfree in
    the sense of \ref{def:quantum-loop-free}. \( \widetilde{A} \) is
    called \emph{undirected} if \( \widetilde{A}^{*}_{g} =
    \widetilde{A}_{g^{-1}} \) holds for all \( g \in G \).
\end{definition}

We can connect the notions of voltage and derived graphs to their quantum

\begin{definition}[Pre-simple voltage graph]
    A voltage graph \( (\Gamma, \lambda) \) is called \emph{pre-simple} if
    its derived graph \( \Gamma^{\lambda} \) is simple.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[\textcite{tobolski26}, Proposition 4.10]
    \label{prop:equivalence}
    Let \( G \) be a finite abelian group, and let
    \( (\mathbb{C}^V, \psi_V) \) be a quantum set. Given a
    pre-simple voltage graph \( (\Gamma,\lambda) \) on \( V \) with
    labelling \( \lambda \colon E \to G \), let
    \( \widetilde{A}_g \colon \mathbb{C}^V \to \mathbb{C}^V \) denote the
    adjacency matrix of the (simple, directed) subgraph of \( \Gamma \)
    consisting of exactly those edges labeled \( g \). Then the
    assignment
    \[
        (\Gamma,\lambda) \;\longmapsto\; \widetilde{A} =
        (\widetilde{A}_g)_{g \in G}
    \]
    is a one-to-one correspondence between \( G \)-labeled pre-simple
    voltage graphs on \( V \) and voltage quantum graphs on
    \( (\mathbb{C}^V, \psi_V) \) with respect to the trivial action on
    \( (\mathbb{C}^V, \psi_V) \).
\end{proposition}

In the same manner certain classical derived graphs can be identified
with derived quantum graphs. Note that the group action
in question is trivial, which connects to Theorem~\ref{thm:gross-and-tucker},
which states that a graph is isomorphic to a derived graph of some voltage
graph in and only if it allows a free group action of some fixed finite group.

\begin{proposition}[\textcite{tobolski26}, Proposition 4.12]
    \label{prop:derived-graph-identification}
    In the setting of Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence},
    let \( (\Gamma,\lambda) \) be a pre-simple voltage graph with
    associated voltage quantum graph
    \( \widetilde{A} = (\widetilde{A}_g)_{g \in G} \). Then the derived graphs
    agree under the identification
    \[
        \Gamma^\lambda = \widetilde{A} \rtimes_{\mathrm{triv}} \widehat{G},
    \]
    where \( \Gamma^\lambda \) is the Gross--Tucker derived graph, and
    \( \widetilde{A} \rtimes_{\mathrm{triv}} \widehat{G} \) is the derived
    quantum graph of Definition~\ref{def:quantum-voltage-graph};
    here classical graphs on \( V \times G \) are identified with
    quantum graphs on \( (\mathbb{C}^V,\psi_V)
    \rtimes_{\mathrm{triv}} \widehat{G} \).
\end{proposition}

Thus we can identify classical derived graphs with quantum graphs
on cross-product quantum sets with the trivial action. This will
come useful later as this will allow us to establish a quantum isomorphism
between nonclassical graphs on \( (M_{3}(\mathbb{C}), 3
\operatorname{Tr}) \) and classical
graphs that happen to be derived graphs.

\begin{lemma}[\textcite{tobolski26}, Lemma 4.11]
    \label{lem:correspondences}
    Let \( (\mathbb{C}^V, \psi_V) \) be the quantum set associated with
    a finite set \( V \), and let
    \( G \) be a finite abelian group equipped with the trivial action
    \( \mathrm{triv} \colon \widehat{G} \to
    \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^V, \psi_V) \).
    Then following hold.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The assignment on generators
            \[
                e_v \mapsto \sum_{g \in G} e_{v,g}, \qquad
                u_\chi \mapsto \sum_{v \in V,\, g \in G} \chi(g)\, e_{v,g}
            \]
            extends to an isomorphism
            \[
                (\mathbb{C}^V, \psi_V) \rtimes_{\mathrm{triv}} \widehat{G}
                \;\cong\; (\mathbb{C}^{V \times G}, \psi_{V \times G}),
            \]
            where the right-hand side is the quantum set associated
            to the finite set \( V \times G \) as in
            Example~\ref{ex:classical_sets},
            and \( e_v \), \( e_{v,g} \) denote the indicator functions
            in \( \mathbb{C}^{V} \) and \( C(V \times G) \), respectively.

        \item Under this identification, the operator
            \( X_g \colon B \to B \), \( g \in G \), from
            Definition~\ref{def:quantum-voltage-graph}
            corresponds to the classical graph on \( V \times G \)
            whose edges are exactly
            \[
                (v,h) \to (w, hg), \qquad v,w \in V,\ h \in G.
            \]

        \item Likewise, the operator
            \( E_\rtimes^* \widetilde{A}_g E_\rtimes \colon B \to B \)
            from Definition~\ref{def:quantum-voltage-graph}
            corresponds to the classical graph on \( V \times G \)
            whose edges are exactly
            \[
                (v,h) \to (w,k), \qquad h,k \in G,\ v
                \xrightarrow{\widetilde{A}_g} w,
            \]
            where \( v \xrightarrow{\widetilde{A}_g} w \) means that
            \( v \) and \( w \) are joined by an edge in the classical
            graph corresponding to the adjacency matrix
            \( \widetilde{A}_g \).

        \item Finally, suppose \( A_1, A_2 \) are quantum adjacency
            matrices corresponding, in the sense of
            Example~\ref{ex:classical_sets},
            to classical graphs \( \Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \) on
            \( V \times G \). Then
            \[
                A_3 = m(A_1 \otimes A_2)\, m^*
            \]
            is again a quantum adjacency matrix, and corresponds to
            the classical graph \( \Gamma_3 = \Gamma_1 \cap \Gamma_2 \)
            with vertex set \( V \times G \) and edge set given by the
            intersection of the edge sets of \( \Gamma_1 \) and
            \( \Gamma_2 \).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\section{Isomorphisms and Quantum Isomorphisms of Quantum Sets}
\label{sec:quantum-isomorphisms}

Quantum isomorphisms were first conceived in \textcite{atserias16} in
the context of game theory. We follow the definitions due to
\textcite{tobolski26}, which in turn build on the work of
\textcite{musto18}.

\begin{definition}[Quantum isomorphisms, \textcite{tobolski26},  Definition 2.8]
    \label{def:quantum-isomorphisms}
    Let \( (B_{1}, \psi_{1}) \) and \( (B_{2}, \psi_{2}) \) be
    quantum sets, and let \( A_{1}\colon B_{1} \rightarrow B_{1} \)
    and \( A_{2}\colon B_{2} \rightarrow B_{2} \) be quantum graphs on
    \( (B_{1}, \psi_{1}) \) and \( (B_{2}, \psi_{2}) \), respectively.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The quantum sets \( (B_1,\psi_1) \) and
            \( (B_2,\psi_2) \) are called \emph{isomorphic} if there
            exists a \( * \)-isomorphism \( \phi\colon B_{1}
            \rightarrow B_{2} \)
            satisfying \( \psi_{2} \circ \phi = \psi_{1} \). The group
            of all such self-isomorphisms of a quantum set
            \( (B, \psi) \) is denoted \( \operatorname{Aut}(B, \psi) \).
        \item Two quantum graphs are called \emph{isomorphic} if the
            underlying quantum sets admit an isomorphism
            \( \phi\colon B_{1} \rightarrow B_{2} \) which additionally
            intertwines the graphs, i.e.\ satisfies
            \( \phi A_{1} = A_{2} \phi \). The corresponding group of
            automorphisms of a quantum graph \( A \) is written
            \( \operatorname{Aut}(A) \).
        \item The quantum sets \( (B_1,\psi_1) \) and
            \( (B_2,\psi_2) \) are said to be \emph{quantum isomorphic},
            denoted \( (B_{1}, \psi_{1}) \cong_q (B_{2}, \psi_{2}) \),
            if there exist a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
            \( \mathcal{H} \) together with a unital
            \( * \)-homomorphism
            \[
                \rho\colon B_{1} \rightarrow B_{2} \otimes B(\mathcal{H})
            \]
            for which the induced map
            \[
                p\colon L^{2}(B_{1}) \otimes \mathcal{H} \rightarrow
                L^{2}(B_{2}) \otimes \mathcal{H}, \qquad
                a \otimes \xi \longmapsto \rho(a)(1 \otimes \xi),
            \]
            is a unitary operator between Hilbert spaces.
        \item Two quantum graphs \( A_1, A_2 \) are \emph{quantum
            isomorphic}, written \( A_{1} \cong_{q} A_{2} \), if the
            underlying quantum sets admit a quantum isomorphism
            \( \rho\colon B_{1} \rightarrow B_{2} \otimes B(\mathcal{H}) \)
            that in addition satisfies the intertwining condition
            \[
                \rho A_{1} = (A_{2} \otimes
                \operatorname{Id}_{B(\mathcal{H})}) \, \rho.
            \]
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:quantum-iso-trivial-hilbert-space}
    Taking \( \mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C} \) in the definition above
    recovers the ordinary notion of isomorphism as a special case: a
    unital \( * \)-homomorphism \( \rho \colon B_1 \to B_2 \otimes
    B(\mathbb{C}) = B_2 \) for which \( p = \rho \) is unitary is
    exactly a \( * \)-isomorphism \( \phi \colon B_1 \to B_2 \)
    satisfying \( \psi_2\circ\phi = \psi_1 \), and the intertwining
    condition \( \rho A_1 = (A_2\otimes\operatorname{Id})\rho \)
    reduces to \( \phi A_1 = A_2\phi \). In this sense, quantum
    isomorphisms genuinely generalize the classical notion above.
    % : they
    % replace a single isomorphism \( \phi \) between the quantum sets
    % themselves by a family of maps, twisted by an auxiliary Hilbert
    % space \( \mathcal{H} \), that need only behave like an isomorphism
    % \emph{on average} over \( \mathcal{H} \). This is exactly what allows
    % two quantum graphs to be quantum isomorphic without being
    % isomorphic in the ordinary sense, mirroring how, in the game-theoretic
    % origin of the notion, a quantum strategy can win a non-local game
    % that no classical strategy can win.
\end{remark}

Quantum isomorphism is thus a strictly weaker notion than ordinary
isomorphism: every isomorphism of quantum sets or quantum graphs is in
particular a quantum isomorphism, but not conversely. An example is
given in \ref{prop:quantum-iso-is-weaker}. Later, we will
use this weaker notion to establish quantum isomorphisms between
certain classical derived graphs and quantum graphs on
\( (M_{3}(\mathbb{C}), 3\operatorname{Tr}) \) that are not, and
cannot be, isomorphic
in the ordinary sense.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:quantum-iso-is-weaker}
    Quantum isomorphism \( \cong_q \) is strictly weaker than ordinary
    isomorphism \( \cong \) for quantum sets and quantum graphs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Consider the matrix algebra \( B_1 = M_2(\mathbb{C}) \) and the
    commutative algebra \( B_2 = \mathbb{C}^4 \), equipped with the
    quantum set functionals established earlier in this thesis:
    \[
        \psi_1(x) = 2\operatorname{Tr}(x), \qquad
        \psi_2(y) = \sum_{k=0}^3 y_k.
    \]

    \textbf{Classical non-isomorphism.}
    The \( C^* \)-algebras \( B_1 \) and \( B_2 \) cannot be
    classically isomorphic, since \( B_1 = M_2(\mathbb{C}) \) is
    non-commutative while \( B_2 = \mathbb{C}^4 \) is commutative, and
    any \( * \)-isomorphism would have to preserve commutativity.
    Thus \( (B_1,\psi_1) \not\cong (B_2,\psi_2) \).

    \textbf{Existence of a quantum isomorphism.}
    Set \( \mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^2 \). Let \( \{e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3\} \)
    denote the standard basis of minimal projections of
    \( \mathbb{C}^4 \), and let \( \sigma_0 = I_2 \) together with
    the Pauli matrices \( \sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3 \) of
    Example~\ref{example:pauli-matrices} form the family
    \( \{\sigma_0,\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3\} \subseteq U(2) \).

    Define the unital \( * \)-homomorphism
    \( \rho \colon M_2(\mathbb{C}) \to \mathbb{C}^4 \otimes M_2(\mathbb{C}) \)
    by
    \[
        \rho(x) = \sum_{k=0}^3 e_k \otimes \sigma_k x \sigma_k^*.
    \]
    The induced map
    \( p \colon L^2(M_2(\mathbb{C})) \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 \to
    L^2(\mathbb{C}^4) \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 \) acts as
    \( p(a \otimes \xi) = \rho(a)(1 \otimes \xi) \). Since the
    \( e_k \) are pairwise orthogonal with \( \psi_2(e_k) = 1 \), and
    \( \sigma_k^*\sigma_k = I_2 \), a direct computation gives
    \begin{align*}
        \langle p(a \otimes \xi), p(b \otimes \eta) \rangle
        &= \sum_{k=0}^3 \operatorname{Tr}\big( \xi^* \sigma_k (a^*b)
        \sigma_k^* \eta \big) \\
        &= \operatorname{Tr}\left( \xi^* \left( \sum_{k=0}^3 \sigma_k
        (a^*b) \sigma_k^* \right) \eta \right).
    \end{align*}
    By the Pauli twirl identity \( \sum_{k=0}^3 \sigma_k Y \sigma_k^* =
    2\operatorname{Tr}(Y) I_2 \), valid for any \( Y \in M_2(\mathbb{C}) \),
    this simplifies to
    \[
        \langle p(a \otimes \xi), p(b \otimes \eta) \rangle =
        2\operatorname{Tr}(a^*b)\, \langle \xi, \eta \rangle_{\mathbb{C}^2}
        = \psi_1(a^*b)\, \langle \xi, \eta \rangle_{\mathbb{C}^2}
        = \langle a, b \rangle_{L^2(M_2(\mathbb{C}))}\, \langle \xi,
        \eta \rangle_{\mathbb{C}^2}.
    \]
    Thus \( p \) preserves inner products. Since
    \( \dim(L^2(B_1) \otimes \mathcal{H}) = 4 \times 2 = 8 =
    \dim(L^2(B_2) \otimes \mathcal{H}) \), \( p \) is a unitary operator.

    Taking the quantum graphs \( A_1 = 0 \) on \( (B_1,\psi_1) \) and
    \( A_2 = 0 \) on \( (B_2,\psi_2) \), the intertwining condition
    \( \rho A_1 = (A_2 \otimes \operatorname{Id}) \rho \) holds
    trivially, since both sides vanish.

    Hence \( A_1 \cong_q A_2 \), while
    \( (B_1,\psi_1) \not\cong (B_2,\psi_2) \) rules out
    \( A_1 \cong A_2 \) as well. This proves the claim.
\end{proof}

The theorem below lets us establish quantum isomorphisms between
derived quantum graphs differing only by the choice of group action.

\begin{theorem}[\textcite{tobolski26}, Theorem~5.2]
    \label{thm:quantum-iso-different-actions}
    Let \( (\widetilde{B}, \widetilde\psi) \), the action
    \( \widehat\alpha \colon \widehat{G} \to \operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{B},
    \widetilde\psi) \),
    and the crossed product
    \( (\widetilde{B} \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{G},
    \psi_\rtimes) \) be as
    introduced above, and let \( \widetilde{A} = (\widetilde{A}_g)_{g \in G} \)
    be a voltage quantum graph on \( (\widetilde{B}, \widetilde\psi) \) with
    respect to the action \( \widehat\alpha \) of \( \widehat{G} \). Then the
    corresponding derived quantum graphs satisfy the quantum
    isomorphism
    \[
        \widetilde{A} \rtimes_{\widehat\alpha} \widehat{G}
        \;\cong_q\;
        \widetilde{A} \rtimes_{\mathrm{triv}} \widehat{G}.
    \]
\end{theorem}

What is truly remarkable in \ref{thm:quantum-iso-different-actions}
is that we can start with a classical pre-simple voltage graph with
dual group action that is compatible with the labeling and obtain a
genuine quantum graph, i.e. one that is not isomorphic to any classical graph.

Theorem~\ref{thm:quantum-iso-different-actions} will be central to our
strategy in Section~\ref{sec:classification-loopfree-undirected-m3}
by choosing \( \widehat\alpha \) to be
the trivial action, we already know from
Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence} and
Lemma~\ref{lem:correspondences} that
\( \widetilde{A}\rtimes_{\mathrm{triv}}\widehat{G} \) is (isomorphic to) a
\emph{classical} derived graph. The above theorem then lets us transport
this classical realization back along any other action \( \widehat\alpha \)
we might prefer to work with, establishing a quantum isomorphism
between the (possibly non-classical) derived quantum graph
\( \widetilde{A}\rtimes_{\widehat\alpha}\widehat{G} \) and a genuinely classical
graph, without having to construct that isomorphism by hand in each
individual case. This is precisely the tool we will use to show that
every one of the five equivalence classes of loop-free, undirected
quantum graphs on \( (M_{3}(\mathbb{C}), 3\operatorname{Tr}) \) established in
Corollary~\ref{cor:five-classes} is quantum isomorphic to a classical
graph on nine vertices.

\section{Gromada's theorem}
\label{sec:gromada-theorem}

In this section, we state a theorem due to \textcite{gromada22}
establishing a correspondence between certain linear
subspaces of \( \mathfrak{sl}(n) \) and quantum graphs.
This result will
be used to classify all loop-free and undirected quantum graphs on
\( M_{3}(\mathbb{C}) \) up to quantum isomorphism, and it also
provides an explicit
recipe for recovering the adjacency matrix corresponding to a given
subspace.

\begin{definition}[Quantum Edges]
    Let \( A_V \colon M_n (\mathbb{C}) \to M_n (\mathbb{C}) \) be a
    quantum graph on \( M_n (\mathbb{C}) \)
    associated with a subspace \( V \subseteq
    \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{C}) \). The \emph{number of quantum
    edges} of the quantum graph \( A_V \) is defined as the dimension
    of \( V \):
    \[
        \operatorname{q-edges}(A_V) = \dim(V).
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[\textcite{gromada22}, Theorem 3.7]
    \label{thm:gromada}
    Every linear map \( A \colon M_n (\mathbb{C}) \to M_n
    (\mathbb{C}) \) defining a quantum
    graph on \( M_n (\mathbb{C}) \) corresponds uniquely to a subspace \( V
    \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{C}) \) via
    \[
        A = A_V = n^2 \sum_{s=1}^{\dim(V)} \xi_s \otimes \bar{\xi_s},
    \]
    where \( (\xi_1,\dots,\xi_{\dim(V)}) \) is any orthonormal basis
    of \( V \) with respect to the Hilbert--Schmidt inner product.

    Furthermore, the quantum graph \( A_V \):
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Has \( m = \dim(V) \) quantum edges;
        \item Has no loops if and only if \( V \subseteq
            \mathfrak{sl}(n) \);
        \item Is undirected if and only if \( V = V^* = \{x^* \mid x
            \in V\} \).
    \end{enumerate}

    If two subspaces \( V, W \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{C}) \)
    determine isomorphic quantum graphs \( A_V \cong A_W \), then \(
    V = UWU^* \) for some unitary matrix \( U \in SU(n) \).
\end{theorem}

\begin{def}[Gromada subspace]
    \label{def:gromada-subspace}
    The subspace corresponding to a quantum graph by
    \ref{thm:gromada} will be called its
    \emph{Gromada subspace}.
\end{def}

\begin{remark}
    The statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:gromada} differs from its
    original formulation by a factor of \( n^2 \) in front of the
    sum. This rescaling is necessary to account for the different
    conventions used in the two works.
\end{remark}

Before applying this correspondence, it will be convenient to have a
more concrete handle on subspaces satisfying \( V = V^* \), since this
is precisely the condition singling out undirected quantum graphs. The
next two lemmas establish that such subspaces always admit a basis of
self-adjoint elements, and that this self-adjoint data is in fact
enough to reconstruct \( V \) uniquely.

\begin{lemma}[\textcite{gromada22}, Lemma 3.8]
    \label{lem:self-adjoint-basis}
    Every vector subspace \( V \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{C}) \) with
    \( V = V^* \) admits a self-adjoint basis \( \{\xi_s\} \), i.e.\ with
    \( \xi_s = \xi_s^* \) for every \( s \).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    We construct the desired basis inductively. Suppose we have already
    obtained a linearly independent set of self-adjoint vectors
    \( \{\xi_s\} \) spanning a subspace \( V_1 \subseteq V \). By
    construction, \( V_1 \) is invariant under the involution, i.e.\
    \( V_1 = V_1^* \).

    If \( V_1 \neq V \), let \( V_2 = V_1^\perp \) be the orthogonal
    complement of \( V_1 \) with respect to the standard inner
    product. Since the inner product and the involution are
    compatible, \( V_2 \) is also invariant under the adjoint.
    Choose any nonzero \( \xi \in V_2 \) and decompose it into its
    self-adjoint real and imaginary parts,
    \[
        \xi_{\mathrm{Re}} = \frac{\xi + \xi^*}{2}, \qquad
        \xi_{\mathrm{Im}} = \frac{i(\xi^* - \xi)}{2}.
    \]
    Both \( \xi_{\mathrm{Re}} \) and \( \xi_{\mathrm{Im}} \) are
    self-adjoint elements of \( V_2 \), and since \( \xi \neq 0 \), at
    least one of them is nonzero. We extend our independent set by
    appending either both (if linearly independent) or the single
    nonzero one (if not). Iterating this process eventually produces a
    full self-adjoint basis of \( V \).
\end{proof}

The following lemma shows that any undirected quantum graph on
\( M_n (\mathbb{C}) \) may be identified with a real vector space.

\begin{lemma}[\textcite{gromada22}, Remark 3.10]
    \label{lem:self-adjoint-uniqueness}
    A complex vector space \( V = V^* \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{C})\)
    uniquely determines a real vector space, spanned by a basis
    \( \{\xi_s\} \) of self-adjoint elements \( \xi_s = \xi_s^* \).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Let \( V \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{C}) \) be a vector space with
    \( V = V^* \).

    Since \( V \) is invariant under the Hermitian adjoint, every
    \( v \in V \) admits the decomposition
    \[
        v = \frac{v + v^*}{2} + i\,\frac{v - v^*}{2i}.
    \]
    Both summands lie in \( V \), as \( V \) is closed under addition,
    scalar multiplication, and the adjoint operation, and moreover
    \[
        \left(\frac{v+v^*}{2}\right)^* = \frac{v+v^*}{2}, \qquad
        \left(i\,\frac{v-v^*}{2i}\right)^* = i\,\frac{v-v^*}{2i},
    \]
    so both are self-adjoint.

    Now let \( \xi \in V \) be self-adjoint, and let \( \{\xi_s\} \) be a
    self-adjoint basis of \( V \). Write \( \xi = \sum_s c_s \xi_s \) for
    some \( c_s \in \mathbb{C} \). Taking the Hermitian adjoint gives
    \[
        \xi = \xi^* = \sum_s \overline{c_s}\, \xi_s,
    \]
    since each \( \xi_s \) is self-adjoint, and by uniqueness of the
    expansion with respect to \( \{\xi_s\} \) we get \( c_s = \overline{c_s} \)
    for every \( s \), i.e.\ every coefficient \( c_s \) is real.

    Hence every self-adjoint element of \( V \) is a real linear
    combination of \( \{\xi_s\} \), so the self-adjoint part of \( V \)
    is a real vector space uniquely determined by \( V \).
\end{proof}

By the above lemma, we may regard any self-adjoint
\( V \subseteq \mathfrak{sl}(n) \) as a real subspace of
\( \mathfrak{sl}(n) \).

\begin{remark}[\textcite{gromada22}, Section 2.3]
    \label{rem:counting-vertices-edges}
    For a quantum set \( (B,\psi) \), it is tempting to call the
    minimal central projections of \( B \) (i.e.\ the matrix blocks
    \( M_{n_i} \) in a decomposition \( B \cong \bigoplus_i M_{n_i} \))
    its \emph{vertices}. This works well enough for classical sets,
    where \( B = \mathbb{C}^n \) decomposes into \( n \) copies of
    \( M_1 (\mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C} \), recovering the usual \( n \) vertices.
    However, for a genuinely noncommutative quantum set such as
    \( (M_n (\mathbb{C}), n\operatorname{Tr}) \), this convention is
    misleading: it
    would count \( M_n (\mathbb{C}) \) as a single \emph{vertex}, even though
    \( \dim(M_n (\mathbb{C})) = n^2 \) and the quantum graphs living
    on \( M_n (\mathbb{C}) \)
    behave, in every respect relevant to us, like graphs on \( n^2 \)
    points rather than on a single point.

    This is exactly why we define \( \operatorname{q-edges}(A_V) \)
    as the dimension \( \dim(V) \) of the corresponding subspace
    \( V \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{C}) \), rather than by
    some more naive
    combinatorial count: it is the dimension of the underlying vector
    space, not a literal count of \emph{vertices} or \emph{edges} in
    any classical sense, that yields consistent statements such as
    Corollary~\ref{cor:sixteen-subspaces} and the classification of
    Table~\ref{tab:classification}.
\end{remark}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:summary-quantum-sets-and-graphs}

This chapter laid the algebraic groundwork for treating classical
combinatorial structures and their quantum analogues on equal footing.
The guiding principle throughout has been that a finite set, once
recast as a commutative \( C^{*} \)-algebra with a suitable functional,
admits a natural non-commutative generalization, the quantum set,
and that many further classical notion built on top of a set generalizes
correspondingly. We saw this principle realized concretely: matrix
algebras arose as crossed products of classical quantum sets,
classical voltage and derived graphs embedded as a special
(trivial-action) case of their quantum counterparts, and Gromada's
correspondence translated quantum graphs on \( M_n (\mathbb{C}) \) into
linear-algebraic data, i.e. subspaces of \( \mathfrak{gl}(n,
\mathbb{C}) \), which are amenable to direct computation. Quantum
isomorphisms, which are a generalisation of regular isomorphisms,
will help us relate classical graphs to purely quantum ones in the
next chapter.

summarizes this dictionary between the classical and quantum sides,
