\section{Quantum sets}

Let \( B \) be a \( C^{*} \)-algebra equipped with a faithful positive
functional \( \psi \). By the Gelfand--Naimark theorem, \( B \) can be
realized concretely as a closed \( * \)-subalgebra of the bounded linear
operators \( B(\mathcal{H}) \) on some Hilbert space \( \mathcal{H} \).
When \( B \) is finite-dimensional, this abstract picture simplifies
considerably: rather than passing to \( \mathcal{H} \), we can
identify \( B \) with its own Hilbert space completion \( L^2(B,\psi) \),
by equipping \( B \) itself with the inner product induced by \( \psi \),
\[
    \langle x, y \rangle = \psi(x^{*}y), \qquad x, y \in B.
\]
Since \( \psi \) is faithful, this form is positive definite, and since
\( B \) is already finite-dimensional, it is automatically complete;
hence \( L^2(B,\psi) \) may simply be taken to be \( B \) itself, viewed
as a Hilbert space via this inner product.

This Hilbert space structure is the key extra piece of data that allows
one to speak of adjoints of maps built from \( B \), and in particular of
the adjoint of multiplication. This leads to the central definition of
this thesis, taken directly from \textcite{tobolski26}.

\begin{definition}
    (Quantum set, \textcite[Definition~2.1]{tobolski26})
    A \emph{quantum set} \( (B, \psi) \) is a pair consisting of a
    finite-dimensional \( C^{*} \)-algebra \( B \) and a faithful positive
    functional \( \psi \colon B \rightarrow \mathbb{C} \) such that
    \[
        m m^{*} = \operatorname{Id}_{B}
    \]
    holds, where \( m \colon B \otimes B \rightarrow B \) is the
    multiplication map and \( m^{*} \) is its adjoint with respect to the
    inner products induced by \( \psi \), i.e.\ the unique linear map
    determined by
    \[
        \left\langle m(a \otimes b), c \right\rangle
        = \left\langle a \otimes b, m^{*}(c) \right\rangle_{\psi \otimes \psi}
    \]
    for all \( a, b, c \in B \).
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    We refer to any functional \( \psi \) satisfying the condition in the
    definition above as a \emph{quantum set functional} on \( B \). The
    condition \( mm^{*} = \operatorname{Id}_B \) should be thought of as a
    normalization condition on \( \psi \): not every faithful positive
    functional on a finite-dimensional \( C^{*} \)-algebra makes
    \( (B,\psi) \) into a quantum set, only those compatible with the
    multiplication in this precise sense.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    Recall that, more generally, a \emph{comultiplication} on a
    \( C^{*} \)-algebra \( A \) is a \( * \)-homomorphism
    \[
        \Delta \colon A \rightarrow A \otimes A
    \]
    making the following diagram commute (coassociativity):
    \[
        \begin{tikzcd}
            A \arrow[r,"\Delta"] \arrow[d,"\Delta"']
            & A\otimes A \arrow[d,"{\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id}}"] \\
            A\otimes A \arrow[r,"{\mathrm{id}\otimes\Delta}"']
            & A\otimes A\otimes A
        \end{tikzcd}
    \]
    In general such a \( \Delta \) is far from unique, and need not
    interact with any given inner product on \( A \) at all. In our
    setting, however, the situation is much more rigid: once
    \( (B,\psi) \) is a quantum set, the adjoint \( m^{*} \) of the
    multiplication map is automatically a comultiplication on \( B \),
    and it is the \emph{unique} one arising this way, since it is pinned
    down by \( m \) together with the Riesz representation theorem for
    the Hilbert space \( L^2(B,\psi) \).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    Because in our setup the comultiplication is obtained from the
    ordinary multiplication \( m \) purely by adjunction with respect to
    \( \psi \), we will not introduce separate notation for it, and will
    simply write \( m^{*} \) throughout.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Classical finite sets as quantum sets]
    Every finite set \( X \) gives rise to a quantum set
    \( (C(X), \psi_X) \), where \( C(X) \) denotes the \( * \)-algebra of
    complex-valued functions on \( X \) with pointwise multiplication and
    involution given by pointwise complex conjugation,
    \( f^*(x) = \overline{f(x)} \), and where
    \[
        \psi_X \colon C(X) \rightarrow \mathbb{C}, \qquad
        \psi_X(f) = \sum_{x \in X} f(x).
    \]

    To see that \( \psi_X \) is positive, note that for any
    \( f \in C(X) \) the product \( f^*f \) evaluates pointwise as
    \[
        (f^*f)(x) = \overline{f(x)}\,f(x) = |f(x)|^2,
    \]
    so that
    \[
        \psi_X(f^*f) = \sum_{x \in X} |f(x)|^2 \geq 0.
    \]

    For faithfulness, suppose \( \psi_X(f^*f) = 0 \) for some
    \( f \in C(X) \), i.e.
    \[
        \sum_{x \in X} |f(x)|^2 = 0.
    \]
    Since each summand \( |f(x)|^2 \) is non-negative, the sum can vanish
    only if every term does, so \( f(x) = 0 \) for all \( x \in X \), and
    hence \( f = 0 \). Thus \( \psi_X \) is faithful.

    It remains to check the quantum set condition \( mm^* =
    \operatorname{Id} \).
    Let \( \{e_x\}_{x \in X} \) denote the orthonormal basis of \( C(X) \)
    given by the characteristic functions of points, so that
    \[
        m(f \otimes g) = fg, \qquad m^*(e_x) = e_x \otimes e_x.
    \]
    Evaluating the composite \( mm^* \) on a basis element gives
    \[
        (mm^*)(e_x) = m\big(m^*(e_x)\big) = m(e_x \otimes e_x) = e_x e_x = e_x,
    \]
    since \( e_x \) is idempotent. As \( mm^* \) and \( \operatorname{Id} \)
    agree on the basis \( \{e_x\}_{x \in X} \), they agree on all of
    \( C(X) \) by linearity, so \( mm^* = \operatorname{Id} \), and
    \( (C(X), \psi_X) \) is indeed a quantum set.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The special case \( \mathbb{C}^n \)]
    Taking \( X = \{1, \dots, n\} \) in the previous example recovers the
    quantum set \( (\mathbb{C}^n, \psi_n) \), where \( \psi_n \) is
    determined on the standard basis by \( \psi_n(e_i) = 1 \) and extends
    linearly to
    \[
        \psi_n(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i, \qquad x \in \mathbb{C}^n.
    \]
    Positivity and faithfulness follow exactly as above: writing
    \( x = \sum_i x_i e_i \) and using orthogonality of the basis,
    \[
        x^*x = \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n \overline{x_i}\,
        e_i\Big)\Big(\sum_{j=1}^n x_j e_j\Big)
        = \sum_{i=1}^n \overline{x_i}x_i\, e_i = \sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|^2 e_i,
    \]
    so that
    \[
        \psi_n(x^*x) = \sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|^2 \psi_n(e_i) =
        \sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|^2 \geq 0,
    \]
    which vanishes precisely when every \( x_i = 0 \), i.e.\ when
    \( x = 0 \). The quantum set condition again reduces to the
    computation
    \[
        (mm^*)(e_i) = m(e_i \otimes e_i) = e_i, \qquad i = 1,\dots,n,
    \]
    which forces \( mm^* = \operatorname{Id} \) by linearity.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Matrix algebras]
    For any \( n \in \mathbb{N}_+ \), the pair
    \( (M_n, n\operatorname{Tr}) \) is a quantum set, where
    \( \operatorname{Tr} \) denotes the usual trace on \( M_n \). We will
    later see that this quantum set arises naturally as a crossed
    product: writing \( \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \) for the dual of
    \( \mathbb{Z}_n \) acting on \( (\mathbb{C}^n, \psi_n) \) via the
    standard action, one has
    \[
        (M_n, n\operatorname{Tr}) \;\cong\;
        (\mathbb{C}^n \rtimes \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n,\ \psi_\rtimes),
    \]
    a fact we prove in \ref{lem:iso}.
\end{example}

\begin{lemma}[Comultiplication for matrix algebras]
    In the quantum set \( (M_n, n\operatorname{Tr}) \), the
    comultiplication \( m^* \) is given on matrix units by
    \[
        m^*(e_{ij}) = \sum_{k=1}^n e_{ik} \otimes e_{kj},
    \]
    where \( e_{ij} \) denotes the matrix with a single \( 1 \) in the
    \( i \)-th row and \( j \)-th column, extended linearly to all of
    \( M_n \).
\end{lemma}

% TODO: check this proof carefully
\begin{proof}
    The proof is a direct computation using two standard facts about
    matrix units:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \( e_{ab}e_{cd} = \delta_{bc}\, e_{ad} \);
        \item \( \langle e_{ij}, e_{kl} \rangle =
            \operatorname{Tr}(e_{ji}e_{kl}) = \delta_{ik}\,\delta_{jl} \),
    \end{enumerate}
    where \( \delta_{ij} \) is the Kronecker delta. In particular, (2)
    shows that \( \{e_{ij}\}_{i,j=1,\dots,n} \) forms an orthonormal
    basis of \( M_n \) with respect to the inner product induced by
    \( n\operatorname{Tr} \).

    Fix arbitrary basis vectors \( e_{ab}, e_{cd} \). By (1),
    \[
        m(e_{ab} \otimes e_{cd}) = e_{ab}e_{cd} = \delta_{bc}\, e_{ad},
    \]
    and hence, for any basis vector \( e_{ij} \),
    \[
        \big\langle m(e_{ab} \otimes e_{cd}),\, e_{ij} \big\rangle
        = \delta_{bc} \big\langle e_{ad}, e_{ij} \big\rangle
        = \delta_{bc}\,\delta_{ai}\,\delta_{dj}.
    \]
    On the other hand, pairing \( e_{ab} \otimes e_{cd} \) against the
    proposed adjoint gives
    \[
        \Big\langle e_{ab} \otimes e_{cd},\ \sum_{k=1}^n e_{ik}
        \otimes e_{kj} \Big\rangle
        = \sum_{k=1}^n \langle e_{ab}, e_{ik} \rangle \langle e_{cd},
        e_{kj} \rangle
        = \sum_{k=1}^n \delta_{ai}\delta_{bk}\,\delta_{ck}\delta_{dj}
        = \delta_{ai}\,\delta_{bc}\,\delta_{dj},
    \]
    using that the sum over \( k \) collapses to the single term
    \( k = b = c \). The two expressions coincide, so
    \[
        \big\langle m(e_{ab}\otimes e_{cd}), e_{ij} \big\rangle
        = \big\langle e_{ab}\otimes e_{cd}, m^*(e_{ij}) \big\rangle
    \]
    for all basis vectors, and since \( \{e_{ij}\} \) is an orthonormal
    basis, this identifies \( m^* \) as claimed on the whole of \( M_n \)
    by linearity.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Crossed product quantum sets}

\textcite{tobolski26} introduced the notion of a \emph{crossed product
quantum set}. Suppose we are given a finite-dimensional
\( C^{*} \)-algebra \( \tilde{B} \) together with a dual group action
\( \hat\alpha \colon \hat{G} \to \operatorname{Aut}(\tilde{B}) \), where
\( G \) is a finite abelian group. One may then form the crossed
product
\[
    B := \tilde{B} \rtimes_{\hat\alpha} \hat{G}.
\]
If \( \tilde{B} \) is equipped with a faithful functional
\( \tilde\psi \) making \( (\tilde{B}, \tilde\psi) \) a quantum set, one
can construct a corresponding faithful functional on \( B \) making
\( B \) into a quantum set as well, as follows.

\begin{definition}
    (Crossed product quantum set, \textcite[Definition~3.1]{tobolski26})
    Let \( (\tilde{B}, \tilde\psi) \) be a quantum set, and let
    \( \hat\alpha \colon \hat{G} \to \operatorname{Aut}(\tilde{B},
    \tilde\psi) \)
    be an action of the dual of a finite abelian group \( G \). The
    \emph{crossed product quantum set} is defined as
    \[
        (\tilde{B}, \tilde\psi) \rtimes_{\hat\alpha} \hat{G}
        := \big( \tilde{B} \rtimes_{\hat\alpha} \hat{G},\ \psi_\rtimes \big),
    \]
    where \( \tilde{B} \rtimes_{\hat\alpha} \hat{G} \) is the crossed
    product \( C^{*} \)-algebra, and \( \psi_\rtimes \) is the
    functional given by
    \[
        \psi_\rtimes\Big( \sum_{\chi \in \hat{G}} b_\chi u_\chi \Big)
        = n\, \tilde\psi(b_1),
    \]
    with \( n = |\hat{G}| \).
\end{definition}

The proof that this definition is well posed, i.e. that the resulting
object is indeed a quantum set, can be found in
\textcite[Proposition~3.4]{tobolski26}.

\begin{example}
    (\textcite[Example~3.5]{tobolski26})
    If \( \hat\alpha = \operatorname{triv} \) is the trivial action, then
    \[
        \tilde{B} \rtimes_{\hat\alpha} \hat{G} \;\cong\; \tilde{B} \otimes C(G),
    \]
    via the isomorphism \( b_\chi u_\chi \mapsto b_\chi \otimes
    \tilde u_\chi \),
    where \( \tilde u_\chi \in C(G) \) denotes the function
    \( g \mapsto \chi(g) \) on \( G \).
\end{example}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:iso}
    Let \( \hat\alpha \colon \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \to
    \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^n) \)
    be the action given by cyclically shifting coordinates. Then
    \[
        (M_n, n\operatorname{Tr}) \;\cong\; (\mathbb{C}^n
        \rtimes_{\hat\alpha} \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n,\ \psi_\rtimes).
    \]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Identify \( \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \) with \( \mathbb{Z}_n =
    \{0,1,\dots,n-1\} \)
    (with arithmetic taken modulo \( n \)), so that a general element of
    \( \mathbb{C}^n \rtimes_{\hat\alpha} \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \) is written
    \( b = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}_n} b_k u_k \) with \( b_k \in \mathbb{C}^n \),
    and the action is \( \hat\alpha_k(e_i) = e_{i+k} \) on the standard
    basis \( \{e_i\}_{i \in \mathbb{Z}_n} \) of \( \mathbb{C}^n \), as in
    the previous examples.

    We construct an explicit \( * \)-isomorphism
    \( \pi \colon \mathbb{C}^n \rtimes_{\hat\alpha}
    \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \to M_n \)
    as follows. On \( \mathbb{C}^n \), let \( M_{b_k} \) denote the
    diagonal matrix with entries \( b_k(0), \dots, b_k(n-1) \), i.e.\
    \( M_{b_k} = \sum_i b_k(i)\, e_{ii} \) in the matrix unit notation
    of the earlier lemma. Let \( S \) be the cyclic shift matrix defined
    by \( S e_i = e_{i+1} \) for the standard basis vectors \( e_i \) of
    \( \mathbb{C}^n \) (indices mod \( n \)), and set
    \[
        \pi(b) = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}_n} M_{b_k}\, S^k, \qquad
        b = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}_n} b_k u_k.
    \]

    \textbf{\( \pi \) is a \( * \)-homomorphism.}
    A direct computation shows that \( S^k M_f S^{-k} = M_{\hat\alpha_k(f)} \)
    for \( f \in \mathbb{C}^n \), which is exactly the covariance relation
    \( u_k b u_k^* = \hat\alpha_k(b) \) required of the crossed product.
    Using this, for \( a = \sum_k a_k u_k \) and \( b = \sum_l b_l u_l \),
    \[
        \pi(a)\pi(b)
        = \sum_{k,l} M_{a_k} S^k M_{b_l} S^l
        = \sum_{k,l} M_{a_k}\, M_{\hat\alpha_k(b_l)}\, S^{k+l}
        = \sum_{m} M_{\sum_{k+l=m} a_k \hat\alpha_k(b_l)}\, S^{m}
        = \pi(ab),
    \]
    matching the multiplication rule on \( C_c(\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n,
    \mathbb{C}^n, \hat\alpha) \)
    from the previous subsection, and similarly
    \[
        \pi(a)^* = \sum_k S^{-k} M_{a_k^*} = \sum_k
        M_{\hat\alpha_{-k}(a_k^*)}\, S^{-k} = \pi(a^*),
    \]
    matching the involution formula. So \( \pi \) is a \( * \)-homomorphism.

    \textbf{\( \pi \) is bijective.}
    Write \( e_i = \delta_i \in \mathbb{C}^n \) for the standard basis
    vectors. Since \( S^k \) sends \( e_j \mapsto e_{j+k} \), the operator
    \( M_{e_i} S^k \) sends \( e_{i-k} \mapsto e_i \) and annihilates every
    other basis vector, i.e.
    \[
        \pi(e_i u_k) = M_{e_i} S^k = e_{i,\,i-k},
    \]
    a matrix unit in the sense of the earlier lemma. As \( i \) ranges
    over \( \mathbb{Z}_n \) and \( k \) ranges over \( \mathbb{Z}_n \),
    the pair \( (i, i-k) \) ranges exactly once over all of
    \( \mathbb{Z}_n \times \mathbb{Z}_n \) (for fixed \( i \), varying
    \( k \) makes \( i-k \) range over all residues). Hence
    \( \{\pi(e_i u_k)\}_{i,k \in \mathbb{Z}_n} \) is precisely the full
    set of matrix units \( \{e_{ab}\}_{a,b \in \mathbb{Z}_n} \), which
    forms a basis of \( M_n \). Since \( \{e_i u_k\}_{i,k} \) is a basis
    of \( \mathbb{C}^n \rtimes_{\hat\alpha} \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \)
    (of the same dimension \( n^2 \)) and \( \pi \) carries it bijectively
    to a basis of \( M_n \), the linear map \( \pi \) is a bijection.

    \textbf{\( \pi \) intertwines the functionals.}
    Using \( \pi(e_iu_k) = e_{i,i-k} \) and the fact that a matrix unit
    \( e_{ab} \) has trace \( \delta_{ab} \), we get
    \[
        \operatorname{Tr}\big(\pi(e_i u_k)\big) = \delta_{i,\,i-k} =
        \delta_{k,0}.
    \]
    Hence for a general element \( b = \sum_{k} b_k u_k = \sum_{k,i}
    b_k(i)\, e_i u_k \),
    \[
        \operatorname{Tr}(\pi(b))
        = \sum_{k,i} b_k(i)\, \operatorname{Tr}\big(\pi(e_i u_k)\big)
        = \sum_{k,i} b_k(i)\, \delta_{k,0}
        = \sum_i b_0(i)
        = \tilde\psi(b_0),
    \]
    where \( \tilde\psi \) is the quantum set functional
    \( \tilde\psi(x) = \sum_i x_i \) on \( (\mathbb{C}^n, \tilde\psi) \)
    from the earlier example, and \( b_0 \) is precisely the coefficient
    at the trivial character (i.e.\ \( k = 0 \)) appearing in the
    definition of \( \psi_\rtimes \). Multiplying by \( n =
    |\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n| \),
    \[
        n\operatorname{Tr}(\pi(b)) = n\,\tilde\psi(b_0) = \psi_\rtimes(b).
    \]

    Thus \( \pi \) is a \( * \)-isomorphism
    \( \mathbb{C}^n \rtimes_{\hat\alpha} \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n \to M_n \)
    satisfying \( n\operatorname{Tr} \circ \pi = \psi_\rtimes \), which is
    exactly an isomorphism of quantum sets
    \[
        (\mathbb{C}^n \rtimes_{\hat\alpha} \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_n,\ \psi_\rtimes)
        \;\cong\; (M_n,\ n\operatorname{Tr}). \qedhere
    \]
\end{proof}

\section{Quantum graphs}

A simple graph is classically defined as a pair \( (V, E) \) with \( V \)
serving as the \emph{set of vertices} and \( E \subseteq [V]^{2} \)
serving as the \emph{set of edges}. Such a graph could be interpreted
as a matrix \( A = [a_{ij}] \in \{0, 1\}^{n \times n} \) with \(
\{v_{i}, v_{j}\} \in E \Leftrightarrow a_{ij} = 1 \); such a
representation is often called the \emph{adjacency matrix}. The key
property of such matrices is that they are invariant with respect to
entrywise multiplication. In turn we can use this property to
generalise graphs on sets to graphs on quantum sets.

The following definitions are due to \textcite{tobolski26}.

% TODO: Tell where this notion was first introduced
\begin{definition}
    (Quantum adjacency matrix, \textcite[Definition~2.5]{tobolski26})
    Let \( (B, \psi) \) be a quantum set. A \emph{quantum adjacency
    matrix} on this quantum set is a linear map \( A\colon B \rightarrow B
    \) which is Schur-idempotent and *-preserving, by which we mean
    that it satisfies
    \[
        m(A \otimes A)m^{*} = A,
        \qquad
        A(b^{*}) = A(b)^{*}
        \quad \text{for all } b \in B.
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    (Quantum graph, \textcite[Definition~2.5]{tobolski26})
    A \emph{quantum graph} on \( (B, \psi) \) is given by a quantum
    adjacency matrix on this quantum set.
\end{definition}

For all intents and purposes we will identify both notions and
simply call an
appropriate \( A\colon B \rightarrow B \) both a quantum adjancenty matrix and a
quantum graph.

\begin{remark}
    There are other equivalent definitions of a quantum graph, namely those
    based on
    \begin{itemize}
        \item an \emph{edge projection}, i.e. a projection \(
            \tilde{A} \in C(X) \otimes C(X)^{\operatorname{op}} \), see
            \textcite{musto18},
        \item
    \end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
    (\textcite[Definition~4.6]{tobolski26})
    Let \( A \) be a quantum graph on a quantum set \( (B, \psi) \).
    We call the quantum graph
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{loopfree} if \( m(A \otimes
            \operatorname{Id})m^{*} = 0, \)
        \item \emph{undirected} if \( A = A^{*} \), where the adjoint
            is taken with respect to the inner product induced by
            \( \psi \).
    \end{itemize}
    We say that a voltage quantum graph \( \tilde{A} = \{
    \tilde{A}_{g} \}_{g \in G} \) has these properties if the
    same equations hold for \( \sum_{g \in G} \tilde{A}_{g} \),
    respectively.
\end{definition}

\section{Voltage and Derived Quantum Graphs}

In this section we will introduce the notions of voltage
and derived quantum graphs. They were first introduced
by Gross in \textcite{gross74}. Those notions have been extended
to quantum graphs by Bjorn and Tobolski in \textcite{tobolski26}.

\subsection{Classical Voltage Graphs}

Voltage graphs can be understood simply as classical graphs
whose edges have been labeled with elements of some finite group:

% TODO: dać referencję do pracy Grossa i Tuckera
\begin{definition}[Classical voltage graph]
    A \emph{voltage graph} is a pair \( (\Gamma, \lambda) \) consisting
    of (not necesarily simple) finite directed graph \( \Gamma \) with
    vertex set \( V \) and edge set \( E \), together with a labelling
    \( \lambda: E \rightarrow G \) of the edges of \( \Gamma \) by
    elements of a finite group \( G \).
\end{definition}

With every voltage graph one can construct a new graph, called a
\emph{derived graph}, as below.

\begin{definition}[Classical derived graph]
    The \emph{derived graph} \( \Gamma^{\lambda} \) of \( (\Gamma, \lambda) \)
    is a graph with vertex set \( V \times G \) and edge set \( E \times G \).
    Whenever \( e \in E \) is an edge from \( u \) to \( v \) in \( \Gamma \)
    and \( g \in G \) is a group element, then the edge \( (e, g) \) goes from
    \( (u, g) \) to \( (v, g \lambda(e)) \) in \( \Gamma^{\lambda} \).
\end{definition}

This is a major result in the theory of voltage and derived graphs.
It can be found
in \textcite{gross87}, Theorem 2.2.2.

% TODO: Is \Gamma directed?
\begin{theorem}[Gross and Tucker Theorem]
    Let \( \tilde{\Gamma} \) be a graph and let \( G \) be a finite
    group acting freely on \( \tilde{\Gamma} \) by automorphisms.
    Then there exists a voltage graph \( (\Gamma, \lambda) \) with
    edges labeled with elements of \( G \) such that \(
    \tilde{\Gamma} \) is isomprphic to the derived graph of \(
    (\Gamma, \lambda) \).
    The graph \( \Gamma \) is the quotient graph \( \tilde{\Gamma} / G \).
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Generalisation to quantum voltage and derived quantum graphs}

Now we will generalise the Gross-Tucker construction to quantum graphs. This was
first done by Bjorn and Tobolski in \textcite{tobolski26}.

The following definition will introduce some key notions.

% TODO: Maybe replace the dagger?
\begin{definition}
    (\textcite{tobolski26}, Definition 4.1)
    Let \( (\tilde{B}, \tilde{\psi}) \), \( \hat{\alpha}: \hat{G} \to
    \mathrm{Aut}(\tilde{B}, \tilde{\psi}) \), \( (B, \psi_\rtimes) \) and
    \( (u_\chi)_{\chi \in \hat{G}} \subset B \) be as above.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item For every \( g \in G \) let \( X_g := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{\chi
            \in \hat{G}}\overline{\chi(g)} u_\chi u_\chi^\dagger \), i.e.
            \[
                X_g : B \to B, \quad b \mapsto \frac{1}{n} \sum_{\chi
                \in \hat{G}} \overline{\chi(g)} \langle u_\chi, b
                \rangle_{\psi_\rtimes} u_\chi.
            \]
        \item A \emph{voltage quantum graph} on the quantum set
            \( (\tilde{B}, \tilde{\psi}) \) with respect to the group
            action \( \hat{\alpha}: \hat{G} \to \mathrm{Aut}(\tilde{B},
            \tilde{\psi}) \) is a family \( \tilde{A} = (\tilde{A}_g)_{g
            \in G} \) of quantum adjacency matrices that is indexed by
            \( G \) and satisfies
            \[
                \tilde{A}_g \hat{\alpha}_\chi = \hat{\alpha}_\chi \tilde{A}_g
            \]
            for all \( g \in G \) and \( \chi \in \hat{G} \).
        \item The \emph{derived quantum graph} \( \tilde{A}
            \rtimes_{\hat{\alpha}} \hat{G}: B \to B \) on \( (B,
            \psi_\rtimes) \) is then given by
            \[
                \tilde{A} \rtimes_{\hat{\alpha}} \hat{G} := \sum_{g
                \in G} m(X_g \otimes E_\rtimes^\ast \tilde{A}_g
                E_\rtimes) m^\ast,
            \]
            where we write  \( m \) for the multiplication map on \(
            B \), i.e. \( m = m_\rtimes \).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The fact that \( X_{g} \) is an adjacency matrix on the quantum set
\( (B, \psi) \) is checked in \textcite{tobolski26}, Proposition 4.5.

% TODO: Moze to wyjebac?
\begin{lemma}
    If \( A, B \in M_n(\{0,1\}) \) and \( m^*: \mathbb{C}^n
    \to \mathbb{C}^n \otimes \mathbb{C}^n \) is the adjoint of the
    multiplication map \( m \), then \( m(A \otimes B)m^* = A \odot B
    \), the entrywise (Hadamard) product of \( A \) and \( B \).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Let \( \{e_1, \dots, e_n\} \) be the standard basis of \(
    \mathbb{C}^n \). The componentwise multiplication map \( m \) acts as:
    \[
        m(e_i \otimes e_j) = \delta_{ij} e_i
    \]
    By definition of the adjoint via the standard inner product, \(
        \langle m(e_i \otimes e_j), e_k \rangle = \langle e_i \otimes
    e_j, m^*(e_k) \rangle \). Since the left-hand side is \(
    \delta_{ij}\delta_{ik} \), it follows that:
    \[
        m^*(e_k) = e_k \otimes e_k
    \]
    We expand the action of the matrices \( A \) and \( B \) on a
    basis vector \( e_k \) via their entries \( A_{ik}, B_{jk} \):
    \[
        Ae_k = \sum_{i=1}^n A_{ik} e_i, \quad Be_k = \sum_{j=1}^n B_{jk} e_j
    \]
    Applying the composite operator \( m(A \otimes B)m^* \) to \( e_k \) yields:
    \[
        m(A \otimes B)m^*(e_k) = m(Ae_k \otimes Be_k)
    \]
    \[
        = m\left( \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ik} B_{jk} (e_i
        \otimes e_j) \right)
    \]
    \[
        = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ik} B_{jk} \delta_{ij} e_i
    \]
    \[
        = \sum_{i=1}^n (A_{ik} B_{ik}) e_i
    \]
    Thus, the \( (i,k) \)-th entry of the matrix \( m(A \otimes B)m^*
    \) is exactly \( A_{ik} B_{ik} \). Because \( A \) and \( B \)
    are binary, this entry evaluates to \( 1 \) if and only if \(
    A_{ik} = 1 \) and \( B_{ik} = 1 \), and \( 0 \) otherwise.
\end{proof}

\section{Isomorphisms and Quantum Isomorphisms of Quantum Sets}

Quantum isomorphisms were first conveived in \textcite{atserias16} in
the context of game theory. We will follow the definitions due to
\textcite{tobolski26}, which in turn follow the work of \textcite{musto18}.

\begin{definition}
    (Quantum isomorphisms, \textcite[Definition~2.8]{tobolski26})
    Let \( (B_{1}, \psi_{1}) \) and \( (B_{2}, \psi_{2}) \) be
    quantum sets, and let \( A_{1}\colon B_{1} \rightarrow B_{1} \)
    and \( A_{2}\colon B_{2} \rightarrow B_{2} \)
    be quantum graphs on \( (B_{1}, \psi_{1}) \) and \( (B_{2},
    \psi_{2}) \), respectively.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The quantum sets \( (B_1,\psi_1) \) and \( (B_2,\psi_2) \)
            are called \emph{isomorphic} if there exists
            a \( * \)-isomorphism
            \( \phi\colon B_{1} \rightarrow B_{2} \) satisfying \( \psi_{2}
            \circ \phi = \psi_{1} \).
            The group of all such self-isomorphisms of a quantum set
            \( (B, \psi) \) is denoted \( \operatorname{Aut}(B, \psi) \).
        \item Two quantum graphs are called \emph{isomorphic} if the
            underlying quantum sets admit an isomorphism
            \( \phi\colon B_{1} \rightarrow B_{2} \) which additionally
            intertwines the graphs, i.e.\ satisfies
            \( \phi A_{1} = A_{2} \phi \). The corresponding group of
            automorphisms of a quantum graph \( A \) is written
            \( \operatorname{Aut}(A) \).
        \item The quantum sets \( (B_1,\psi_1) \) and \( (B_2,\psi_2) \)
            are said to be \emph{quantum isomorphic}, denoted
            \( (B_{1}, \psi_{1}) \cong_q (B_{2}, \psi_{2}) \), if
            there exist a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
            \( \mathcal{H} \) together with a
            unital \( * \)-homomorphism
            \[
                \rho\colon B_{1} \rightarrow B_{2} \otimes B(\mathcal{H})
            \]
            for which the induced map
            \[
                p\colon L^{2}(B_{1}) \otimes \mathcal{H} \rightarrow
                L^{2}(B_{2}) \otimes \mathcal{H}, \qquad a \otimes \xi
                \longmapsto \rho(a)(1 \otimes \xi),
            \]
            is a unitary operator between Hilbert spaces.
        \item Two quantum graphs \( A_1, A_2 \) are \emph{quantum
            isomorphic}, written \( A_{1} \cong_{q} A_{2} \), if the
            underlying quantum sets admit a quantum isomorphism
            \( \rho\colon B_{1} \rightarrow B_{2}
            \otimes B(\mathcal{H}) \) that in addition satisfies the
            intertwining condition
            \[
                \rho A_{1} = (A_{2} \otimes
                \operatorname{Id}_{B(\mathcal{H})}) \, \rho.
            \]
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}
